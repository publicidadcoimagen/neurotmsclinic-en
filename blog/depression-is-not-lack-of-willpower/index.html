Depression is not lack of willpower: what TMS is and how it can support

Quick answer: depression is not lack of willpower, lack of effort, or something that goes away just by trying harder. It is a mental health condition that can affect mood, sleep, energy, concentration, and the desire to do things once enjoyed. Transcranial magnetic stimulation, TMS, is a non-invasive mental health support technique. It may support in some cases, it does not promise a cure, results vary by person, and only the physician determines, in an initial evaluation, whether it may support in each case.

What depression is, in general terms

Talking about depression is talking about something more than a sad day. Many people describe persistent sadness, a loss of interest, tiredness that does not go away with sleep, difficulty concentrating, changes in appetite or sleep, and a feeling that everything takes more effort. These changes can affect work, family, studies, and daily life, and they can last weeks or more. It is not a choice, it is not weakness, and it is not something a person can simply switch off with willpower.

It is also important to say this clearly: depression has many possible causes and many ways of showing up. There is no single cause, no single test that diagnoses it on its own, and no single treatment that works the same for everyone. That is why a professional evaluation is the first step, and not an online questionnaire, a video, or this article. This article is educational: it aims to give clear information so you can arrive at an evaluation with questions and realistic expectations, not to diagnose or replace an evaluation.

Why saying “just try harder” does not help

When someone is going through depression, hearing that they just need to try harder can make them feel more alone, more guilty, or less willing to ask for help. Not because people say it with bad intentions, but because that phrase reduces a health problem to an attitude problem. The help that does work, in general, is listening without judging, supporting without pressuring, encouraging professional care, and respecting the person's pace. If you are going through this, asking for help is not weakness. If you know someone who is going through this, supporting them without minimizing what they feel is already important help.

What TMS is

Transcranial magnetic stimulation is a non-invasive neuromodulation technique. In simple terms, it uses magnetic pulses to stimulate specific areas of the brain, in sessions scheduled at the clinic. It is not surgery, it does not require general sedation, and it does not involve implanting anything. It is applied with specialized equipment, following a protocol defined by the physician, after an evaluation.

TMS has been studied as mental health support, particularly for depression and for other topics such as anxiety, under specific protocols. That does not mean it is for everyone, that it works the same in all cases, or that it replaces other treatments. It means that, in some cases and under medical evaluation, it can be a support option within a broader plan, which may include medical follow-up, psychotherapy, or other care, depending on each person.

What a session is like, step by step

First, an initial evaluation. In that evaluation, the case is heard, the history, symptoms, previous treatments, and health conditions are reviewed, and it is explained clearly whether TMS may support or whether another path is better. Without an evaluation, it cannot be known whether it is a suitable option. Then, if the physician determines it, a session plan is scheduled. Each session lasts 50 minutes, at the clinic. During the session, the person is awake, seated, and the equipment applies the pulses according to the protocol. After the session, the person can resume their usual activities, following the physician's instructions.

The number of sessions, their frequency, and the protocol depend on each case and are defined in the evaluation, not in this article. An exact number of sessions for a result cannot be promised either, because results vary by person, by history, by adherence, and by other factors that are only known in the evaluation.

What it can support and what it does not promise

TMS may support, in some cases, improving symptoms related to mood, energy, or concentration, as part of a supervised plan. That is different from promising a cure, promising that it will work in all cases, or promising that it will replace a treatment the person already follows. This article makes none of those promises, and no serious article should make them without knowing the case.

It is also important not to stop a current treatment because of an article. If you already follow a treatment, talk to your physician before changing, stopping, or combining it with another option. Changing a treatment without supervision can be risky, even with good intentions.

Common myths about depression and TMS

  • “Depression is lack of willpower.” No. It is a mental health condition that deserves professional care.
  • “TMS is surgery or painful by definition.” No. It is a non-invasive technique, at the clinic, without surgery and without general sedation, and the experience varies by person.
  • “After a few sessions I will be cured.” That cannot be promised. Results vary and are assessed in follow-up.
  • “If it does not work for me, it is because I did not try hard enough.” No. The response to a treatment depends on many factors, and it is not a judgment about the person.
  • “I do not need an evaluation, I can start directly.” No. First an evaluation is done, and only the physician determines whether TMS may support in each case.

When to seek help soon

If you or someone close to you has thoughts of harming themselves, seek professional help immediately, go to an emergency service, or call local emergency services. Do not wait for a scheduled appointment. If symptoms worsen quickly, if there are sudden changes in behavior, or if the person cannot take care of themselves, it is also wise to seek care soon. Asking for help in time is part of care, not an exaggeration.

What to expect from your evaluation

In an evaluation you can expect questions about your symptoms, since when you have noticed them, how they affect your daily life, what treatments you have tried, and what you hope for from the process. You can also expect a clear explanation: whether TMS may support in your case, what the plan would be like, how long each session lasts, what it costs, and what follow-up would be done. And you can expect your own questions: bring a list, write down your doubts, and do not settle for an explanation you do not understand. A good evaluation feels like a conversation, not a sale.

Depression in daily life, beyond the diagnosis

For many people, depression does not look like in the movies. It looks like getting up with great effort, like postponing messages, like losing interest in things that used to be enjoyable, like irritability, like difficulty concentrating at work or school, or like a constant feeling of tiredness. Sometimes it looks like apparent functioning on the outside and exhaustion on the inside. That is why judging from the outside, by how a person looks, does not say how they feel inside, and why a professional evaluation, with time and questions, is different from a quick opinion.

We also need to talk about the environment. Support from family, work, and community matters, and it is not just about saying positive phrases. It is about accompanying to appointments when possible, respecting the treatment, not minimizing symptoms, not comparing one person's pain with another's, and understanding that recovery, when it happens, is usually gradual, with progress and setbacks, not a straight line. A realistic support plan includes follow-up, patience, and periodic review, not a single session or a single conversation.

How to prepare for an evaluation, without self-diagnosing

Preparing does not mean arriving with a diagnosis already set. It means arriving with useful information: since when you have noticed the changes, which symptoms are most frequent, how they affect your sleep, appetite, work, or relationships, what treatments you have tried, what medications you currently take, and what you would like to achieve with the process. That information helps the physician understand your case, and it helps you not forget important questions in the consultation. If you feel embarrassed or nervous talking about these topics, that is normal: professionals who work in mental health are used to listening to these topics with respect and without judgment.

What is worth avoiding before an evaluation is looking for a definitive diagnosis on social media, comparing your case with other people's cases, or starting or stopping treatments on the recommendation of a video or a comment. Information on the internet can help understand general topics, but it does not replace the clinical history, examination, and professional judgment of whoever evaluates you in person.
